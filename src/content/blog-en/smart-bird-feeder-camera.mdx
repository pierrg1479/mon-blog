This smart bird feeder started as a gift for my partner. It took far longer than planned, and it turned into a family project: I also love watching the garden birds with my son.

I also simply liked the idea. I wanted to try Frigate and build my own integration rather than buy something off the shelf.

The goal was simple: know which birds visit, with a photo and a video of every visit, without going through a manufacturer's app. Commercial smart bird feeders like Birdfy or Bird Buddy do this very well, with their own app.

I first tried to run everything on my Home Assistant Green, and it didn't work. Buying a mini PC for a homelab project is what let me finish it. Here is how my smart bird feeder works: a camera films the feeder, Frigate recognizes the bird and its species locally, and every visit lands in Slack with a photo and a video clip.

I learned the hard way: plenty of mistakes and a lot of iterations before getting a result I'm happy with. This guide covers everything, step by step: where to put the feeder and the camera, how to install and connect them, the settings for filming birds, and a troubleshooting section with every mistake I made.

## Why didn't I buy a Bird Buddy or a Birdfy?

Because I wanted to build my own smart bird feeder with Frigate, and I had what it takes: a mini PC bought for my homelab, on which Frigate identifies species locally. Recognition happens at home, with no subscription, in exchange for doing the setup yourself.

A commercial feeder goes up and works with the manufacturer's app. It's simpler, but I wouldn't have learned anything, and I couldn't have plugged the visits into my own tools (Notion, Slack, a monthly summary).

If you're starting from scratch, with no server, the math is different: you need to add the cost of a mini PC.

## The hardware I used

- **Camera**: [Reolink Lumus Pro](https://reolink.com/fr/product/lumus-pro/), a mains-powered Wi-Fi camera, €60
- **Hub**: Reolink Hub 1 Lite, which relays the camera's video stream, €50. In my setup it's essential: the camera rejected Frigate's credentials when accessed directly (error 401), while the hub accepts them.
- **Close-up lenses**: a set of 37 mm macro filters (+1, +2, +4, +10), €15.40. I use the +4.
- **Wi-Fi**: a Deco mesh access point added in the garden shed, right next to the camera
- **Server**: a NiPoGi mini PC running Proxmox, €200, bought for my homelab. Frigate runs there in a container, alongside everything else.
- **Home Assistant Green**: it hosts Mosquitto, the MQTT broker that carries Frigate's events
- **Software**: [Frigate](https://frigate.video/) (free, open source), n8n for automation, Notion for the history, Slack for notifications
- **Feeder**: [Garden Locker wall-mounted feeder](https://www.amazon.fr/dp/B091CZ8WS3), steel, with a roof, a perch and a large tray, €38.

## How to set up a smart bird feeder camera, step by step

### Where should you put a bird feeder camera?

Pick a spot with mains power within reach and a good Wi-Fi signal, with the camera lens 15 to 20 cm (6 to 8 in) from the perch, where the bird lands. Also choose a background that doesn't move much: trees or a hedge behind the feeder keep triggering motion detection. And avoid a cage feeder: the bars get in the way of detection.

My first setup placed the camera facing a cage feeder, with vegetation behind it: Frigate didn't detect a single bird there, and the moving trees kept its CPU busy for nothing. Today an open wall-mounted feeder is fixed against the garden shed, and the camera is on the side of the feeder, facing the perch. I had to add a Wi-Fi access point in the shed, and mask the trees and the rosemary moving in the background in Frigate.

### Install and connect the feeder and the camera

1. Mount the feeder on a wall or a support: mine is mounted against the garden shed.
2. Mount the camera on the side of the feeder, facing the perch, with its lens 15 to 20 cm (6 to 8 in) from where the bird lands and the +4 close-up lens in front of it.
3. Plug the camera into the mains.
4. In the Reolink app, connect the camera to Wi-Fi and add it to the hub.
5. In the Deco app, set the nearest access point as the camera's preferred one.
6. In Frigate, add the camera using the hub's RTSP stream (port 554), then draw the zone and the mask.
7. Enable bird classification in Frigate and connect n8n to MQTT for the Notion record and the Slack message.

### What settings should you use to film birds?

On the camera side, a 2560 × 1440 stream at 15 frames per second for recording, and the small 640 × 360 stream for detection. On the Frigate side, a zone limited to the feeder, a mask over whatever moves, bird detection thresholds of 0.5 (the default) and 0.65 (instead of 0.7), and a species kept only above 90% confidence.

Each setting is detailed in the sections below: the Frigate configuration just after, and the video stream in the troubleshooting section.

## How does the smart bird feeder work?

The camera films the feeder continuously, Frigate spots birds in a defined zone and then identifies their species with a model that runs locally. At the end of each visit, n8n creates a record in Notion and posts the photo and a video clip in Slack.

A visit, step by step:

1. The camera sends two streams to the Reolink hub: a small 640 × 360 stream for detection and a 2560 × 1440 stream for recording.
2. Frigate analyzes the small stream at 5 frames per second and looks for the "bird" object in the feeder zone.
3. When a bird is seen, Frigate passes it to its [bird classification model](https://docs.frigate.video/configuration/bird_classification), a MobileNet trained on iNaturalist that knows 964 species. It runs on the CPU, offline once downloaded. The species is only kept if the model is at least 90% sure, the default threshold.
4. At the end of the visit, Frigate publishes the event over MQTT.
5. n8n receives the event, creates a record in Notion (species, photo, date, score) and posts the photo and a clip of up to 25 seconds in Slack.
6. On the last day of the month, a second n8n workflow posts the monthly summary.

### Configuring Frigate for birds

Three settings make all the difference: a zone covering the feeder and the perch, a mask over whatever moves around it (trees, rosemary), and a detection threshold lowered slightly for small birds: 0.65 instead of the default 0.7. The first threshold, 0.5, stays at its default value.

A trap I hit with Frigate 0.17, before moving to 0.18: zone coordinates were compared as text. For the edges of the image, `1.000` was read as pixels and broke the config: write `0` and `1` without decimals, not `0.000` or `1.000`. The other points keep their decimals.

Then you need to turn on species recognition. In Frigate, it's under Settings > Enrichments > Object classification, in the "Bird classification config" block: a switch to turn on, and the minimum score, 0.9 by default. You need an internet connection only once, to download the model.

### The n8n workflow: one Notion record and one Slack message per visit

n8n listens to Frigate's MQTT events and only keeps finished visits where Frigate assigned a species. No species, nothing is created: it's the most effective filter against false alerts (leaves, shadows, a cat).

For each visit it keeps, the workflow:

1. checks that no record for the same species was created in the last 30 minutes, to avoid duplicates;
2. fetches the photo from Frigate and creates the record in the Notion database;
3. waits 30 seconds for Frigate to finish the video, then fetches a clip of up to 25 seconds;
4. posts a short message in Slack with the photo, the clip and a button to the full visit in Frigate.

## Troubleshooting: the mistakes I made

I started this project early in the year and only got a satisfying result several months later. In between, I had to fix hardware, installation and configuration problems. Five of them took most of the time: a Home Assistant Green too small, weak Wi-Fi in the garden shed, trees moving behind the feeder, a cage feeder, and blurry birds.

### The Home Assistant Green: not powerful enough, and above all not enough storage

My first instinct was to run everything on the Home Assistant Green. It isn't powerful enough to analyze video continuously, and above all it doesn't have enough storage for the recordings.

The Home Assistant Green only has 32 GB of eMMC storage ([official page](https://www.home-assistant.io/green/)), and with everything already installed on mine, I only had 8 GB free. For scale: in 1080p, my videos weighed about 130 MB per minute of bird, enough to fill those 8 GB in barely an hour of visits. Even on the mini PC, I had to limit video retention to 3 days. The project only really took off with the mini PC bought for my homelab.

### Weak Wi-Fi in the garden shed

The camera is near the garden shed, where the Wi-Fi was too weak and caused disruptions. So I added a Deco mesh access point in the shed.

That wasn't enough right away. The first videos were choppy, with gaps of several seconds: the camera stayed connected to a distant access point, with 1.7% packet loss and spikes up to 460 ms. Setting the shed access point as the preferred one in the Deco app brought it down to 0% loss and 12 ms on average.

### Framing: trees moving in the background

Behind the feeder there are trees, a hedge and a rosemary bush. With the wind, Frigate saw motion 61% of the time and ran 17.2 analyses per second for a 5 fps stream: it examines several regions of each frame where something moves. Its CPU usage sat at 37%, for nothing.

A motion mask over the trees and a zone limited to the feeder changed everything: 0.1 to 10.4 analyses per second, and CPU usage between 11 and 22%, over a first measurement of a few minutes, at night. I had to redo the mask every time I moved the camera.

### The cage feeder: no birds detected

My first feeder was a cage feeder, and I had moved the camera to about 30 cm (12 in) from it to make the bird bigger in the frame. Result: zero detections in 7 days. Two likely causes: the bars of the cage, which partly hide the bird, and the focus (in a daytime snapshot, the feeder was blurry and the background sharp).

I replaced it with the Garden Locker wall-mounted feeder, which is open, fixed against the garden shed. The next day, I fitted the wooden rod that comes with the feeder as a perch, put seeds back, and widened the Frigate zone to cover the whole perch. An hour later, the first great tit was identified, with a 97% score, without any close-up lens. The perch is what mattered most: the birds finally had a clear spot to land, facing the camera.

My advice: avoid cage feeders, and provide a clearly visible perch that sits entirely inside the Frigate zone.

### Blurry birds: from the +1 lens to the +4

Once the new feeder was up, Frigate recognized the birds, but the image stayed blurry: about 150 visits were identified with blurry birds before I fitted the right lens. So the lens didn't unlock detection: it makes the bird sharp.

The Lumus Pro has a fixed focus set for distance: a bird a few dozen centimeters from the lens falls outside the sharp zone. The fix: a close-up lens in front of the camera lens.

I first bought a +1 lens on its own: wasted money, the bird stayed blurry. I then bought a full set (+1, +2, +4, +10), and the +4 is the right one. The best choice depends on the distance between the lens and where the bird lands:

- **+4** if the bird lands 15 to 20 cm (6 to 8 in) from the lens, with a sharp zone of about 5 cm (2 in);
- **+2** between 35 and 50 cm (14 to 20 in);
- **+10**: avoid it, the sharp zone shrinks to about 1 cm.

With the +4, the seeds on the tray are sharp and the background is blurred. Exactly the effect I wanted.

### Video storage

**Recording on motion.** To understand why no bird was being detected, I had Frigate record everything that moved. With the trees, the hedge and insects in front of the infrared at night, Frigate wrote 2 to 3 GB per hour. The disk climbed to 89% and only kept 8 hours of video. That night's backup contained 17.6 GB of night-time video, without a single bird.

**Video size.** In 1080p, my videos reached about 17 Mbit/s. In a single day, I already had 7.5 GB of video for 60 minutes of visits. I cut retention to 3 days and only keep the moments when a bird is visible.

Oversized videos also crashed n8n. A robin staying several minutes means a clip of several hundred MB (over 500 MB for a 4-minute visit): n8n ran out of memory and restarted, with no record and no message. Since then, n8n only asks Frigate for a clip of 25 seconds at most (about 60 MB with my old 1080p setting), and a button links to the full visit.

### Sharper videos by changing two settings

At first, my bird videos looked a bit mushy as soon as the bird moved. The camera recorded in 4K (H.265) at about 4 Mbit/s. Frigate then had to scale the image down to 1080p and re-encode it to H.264 so a browser could play it: two compressions in a row.

I changed two things:

1. In the Reolink app (camera settings > Display > Stream > Clear), I set 2560 × 1440, 4096 kbit/s and 15 fps. At this resolution, the Lumus Pro sends H.264 directly.
2. In Frigate, go2rtc now copies this stream as is instead of re-encoding it.

The video is now compressed once instead of twice. It's kept at 1440p instead of 1080p, and the mini PC's GPU is almost idle. I stayed at 15 frames per second: with a capped bitrate, more frames per second would have meant less detail in each frame. Others prefer to go above 15 fps because birds move fast: with a capped bitrate, it's a trade-off between smoothness and detail.

Another effect: with the bitrate capped at 4096 kbit/s, a minute of bird weighs about 30 MB at most, four times less than with my old 1080p setting.

### Species recognition

Before the local model, I tried sending every photo to Claude Haiku, Anthropic's AI, to identify the species: about 930 input tokens and 400 output tokens per photo. I only tested it for one day, with no real bird in front of the camera: it answered correctly, but it cost money for a job Frigate can do on its own. I quickly switched to Frigate's local model, which is free and identifies the species without sending the image to an external service.

The local model sometimes gets confused, in an easy-to-spot way. Of 13 species recorded in just under two weeks, 8 don't live in a French garden: Carolina chickadee, cedar waxwing, black phoebe, eastern bluebird... These are North American birds, the model's confusion on poorly seen birds. They only account for 11 records out of 171. The monthly summary leaves them out of the ranking and doesn't mention them: they stay visible in Notion.

Another surprise: with Frigate 0.18, the species name changed from "Latin name (English name)" to the English name only. My French messages started announcing "Great Tit". To get the French name back, n8n works in two steps. It first finds the Latin name from the English one, using the species file that ships with Frigate's model. Then it looks up the French name: first in my list of 47 garden birds, otherwise in a table covering the model's 964 species.

That complete table came late. Before, a species missing from my list kept its English name: after ten days of visits, 10 records out of 154 showed up that way, such as "Hermit Thrush". The names mostly come from iNaturalist (835) and Wikidata (86), and the remaining 43 were filled in by hand, favoring the names used in France. The workflow generator now refuses to run if a single French name is missing.

Finally, Frigate splits a visit into several segments as soon as it loses the bird for a few seconds: a robin that stayed 4 minutes produced 9 segments. Two settings solved it: keeping the bird for 10 seconds when it disappears (`max_disappeared`) and closing a visit only after 2 minutes without a bird (`cutoff_time`).

For the first few weeks, I was mostly disappointed: blurry images and detection I couldn't rely on. Today, I'm pretty proud of the result. The feeder runs reliably, and I check the visits on the dedicated Slack channel whenever I have a moment.

What I like most is sharing it with my family. Seeing a robin show up in the middle of the day is pure feel-good.

What still bugs me? Not much: everything runs smoothly and I'm happy with the result. A few birds are still blurry sometimes, and it took quite a few tries with the camera position and the lenses before getting a decent result.

## What I took away from it

This smart bird feeder took much longer than planned, and almost all of that time went into the hardware: the location, the feeder, the Wi-Fi, the focus. If I did it again, I'd start with the image: an open feeder with a perch, a mains-powered camera close to the perch, with the right lens, and a background that doesn't move much.

Today it runs without me thinking about it. Visits land in Slack, the summary arrives at the end of the month, and I watch the garden birds with my son. The gift was a little late, but the whole family enjoys it.
